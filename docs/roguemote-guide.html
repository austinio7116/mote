01The screen

The Thumby Color gives you 128×128 pixels. Roguemote spends 128×104 of them on the map — sixteen tiles across, thirteen down — and the remaining 24 rows on a status strip and two lines of log. Nothing else is ever on screen during play, which is why the log is worth reading: it is the only place the game tells you what just happened.

Health
The red bar and the number beside it. It mends every sixteen turns — a point, plus another for every 64 points of maximum health, so mending a deep character takes about as long as mending a shallow one rather than ten times longer. A ring of regeneration shortens the interval to five turns and doubles what you eat.
Mana
The blue bar. Every cast spends it whether or not the spell succeeds, and it returns on the same sixteen-turn clock as health — a point plus one per 32 of maximum. A big pool is hundreds of turns from empty to full, so a fight is fought with the mana you walked in with.
Level
Your character level, after L. Experience comes from kills. Every level raises maximum health and mana, and better than half the time it raises a stat as well — weighted to what the class is for, so a Warrior thickens and a Mage sharpens, but neither is locked out of the rest. Stats stop at 24.
Depth
TOWN on the surface, DL and a number below it. Depth is difficulty: everything scales with it.
State
A * when you are close to starving, a > while you are hastened.

Levelling stops the game and shows you what it bought: the two bars, any stat that moved, and any spell that just came within reach. It used to be a single line in the log, which meant the most consequential thing that happens to a character was also the least legible — two turns later it had scrolled away.

02Controls

Nine buttons, and a roguelike traditionally wants forty. The compromise is that A is context-sensitive: it does the one sensible thing available where you are standing, and when there is nothing to use it hands you the ring instead.

D-pad

Move one tile. Walking into something attacks it. Hold a direction to keep walking.

LB + D-pad

Move diagonally. The Mines are eight-way; corridors are not.

A

Use what you are standing on: stairs, a shop door, an inn door, a bed, a chest, a cave mouth, a tower — or pull a lever.

D-pad into a keeper

Trade. A shopkeeper is a person standing behind a counter, not a tile you step on, so you walk up and bump into them the way you would anything else — and unlike anything else, that opens their stock instead of hitting them.

A on bare ground

Opens the action ring: four choices parked at the four compass points — Look above you, Map right, Rest below, Pack left. Tap a direction to highlight it, tap the same direction again (or A) to commit, so a stray press never rests you in front of something. Look walks a cursor over anything you have already seen and names it; RB jumps the cursor to the next monster in sight. Rest waits until both bars are full, up to 600 turns — and stops the moment something awake comes into view, or the food runs out.

B

Wait one turn. Useful for letting something come to you in a corridor.

RB

Loose a shot if you are carrying something to shoot, at the nearest thing you can see; otherwise it opens the spell list. Whether a character carries ammunition is a fact about the character rather than about the moment, so the button does not change meaning under you mid-fight.

MENU

Open and close the book: Pack, Gear, Spell, Char, Map. Press it again from any page to get back to play.

LB / RB

Turn to the previous or next page. The tab strip at the top shows where you are.

←

The second action on a page: drop from the Pack, take a slot off in Gear.

04The continent

You start on the surface, on the town, with a hundred and twenty gold and four rations. The continent is generated fresh for each character and does not change afterwards — the same seed rebuilds it every time you surface, so the cave you left is where you left it.

There is no sea. The tileset this game is built from has no water anywhere in it, so rather than ship invented art the island is ringed by impassable rock instead.

Plain

Open ground. Most of the continent.

Forest

Walkable, and it blocks nothing — but you cannot see through a canopy either.

Hills

Walkable highland, drawn with a terraced escarpment along its lower edge.

Mountain

Impassable. The rim of rock around the continent is what makes it an island.

The capital

One per world: a walled compound with gates, streets, all six traders and an inn.

Outlying towns

Two to four more, without walls — a street with houses either side, an inn, and two or three of the same six traders keeping a door. A wall is a thing you build when you are worth attacking.

Cave mouth

The way into the Mines. Ten of them, one always within sight of the capital.

Tower

The other way underground, and the one you can see coming. Its shaft goes deeper the further out it stands, capped at six floors past your best — so the map is a difficulty gradient and a tower is a shortcut, not a pit.

Road

Runs from the capital's gate out to the three nearest cave mouths, so stepping outside you can see a way to go instead of green in every direction.

Ruins

A broken ring of stone with a chest inside. Around twenty a continent.

MENU then LB for the map. On a 128×96 continent seen through a sixteen-tile window it is not a luxury — the yellow mark is the town, the red marks are cave mouths.

05The Mines

You get into the Mines two ways. A cave mouth returns you to the deepest floor you have earned; a tower drops you straight to a floor set by how far out it stands, capped at six past your best. So a tower is a shortcut you can buy with a walk, and the world map is worth reading before you set out.

Each floor is rooms and corridors with a staircase at each end. Floors are not persistent: climbing back up generates a new level at that depth. That is deliberate, and it is what makes going down feel one-way.

Rather more than half the rooms bud straight off a neighbour and are joined by a door through the shared wall, so a floor comes out as clusters of connected chambers with corridors running between the clusters — not one room per corridor. The corridors that remain are not pipes either: they bulge and narrow as they run.

Not every room is a lit rectangle. Some have a corner bitten out, some are halls full of pillars you can lose a monster behind, and some are vaults — a sealed inner chamber behind one closed door, holding chests and something awake that is standing over them. About a third of floors come out as warrens of small cells rather than halls. Deeper floors have more unlit rooms, which have to be walked by torchlight; the room you arrive in is never one of them.

A level is a graph, not a line. Beyond the corridor that chains the rooms together there are one to three extra links, so there is more than one way round and somewhere to run to. Rubble blocks corridors and blocks sight; walking into it clears it and costs you the turn.

Chests come in five colours, poorest to richest: red, blue, gold, green, white. The richer the chest the likelier it is trapped — eight percent on a red, thirty-two on a white. Deep floors roll high tiers more often, but a white chest on floor two is possible, and it is possible on purpose.

Roughly one chest in six is a mimic. There is no way to tell by looking: it draws itself as a real chest, in the colour that tile would have had. It springs when you are within reach.

The walls change as you descend, so the floor you are on is legible at a glance without reading the number.

06Levers

Some floors carry a coloured lever and one thing it opens in the same colour — either a door across a passage, or a strongbox sitting where you can see it and cannot touch it. Stand on the lever and press A. Every lock of that colour gives at once; a freed strongbox becomes an ordinary chest, so you still walk to it, still open it, and it still rolls its trap.

There is never more than one pair on a floor, and that is a safety property rather than a matter of taste. Two pairs can interlock — the red lever behind the blue door and the blue lever behind the red — and no amount of checking each pair on its own would ever see it. With one pair there is nothing to interlock with.

Two more rules keep a floor honest. A locked door only ever replaces a door that was already there, so the generator has proved the level connects through that cell before it locks it. And the lever is placed by flooding the map from the up stair with its own door shut, then dropped somewhere the flood actually reached — so a lever is never behind the thing it opens.

The five are red, blue, gold, green and grey, and the lever band and the strongbox band are drawn in that order, row for row.

07Turns, speed and combat

Nothing moves until you do. Every actor accumulates energy each tick from its speed and acts when it reaches a hundred, which is Moria's model and has one consequence worth internalising: normal speed is 110, and +10 speed is exactly double actions. A potion of speed does not make you 9% faster. It gives you two turns for each one your enemies get, and that is usually the difference between a fight and an escape.

To hit
30, plus three per character level, plus your Dexterity, plus three per point of weapon enchantment, less twice the target's armour class.
Damage
The weapon's dice, plus its enchantment, plus a quarter of your Strength — then multiplied if the weapon has an ego that applies to what you hit.
Blows
One, plus one per twelve Dexterity, plus one more for a weapon of Attacks. Four is the ceiling.
Being hit
Your armour class lowers their chance to connect, and soaks a sixth of anything that does.

Monsters hunt on their own senses, not on yours. Something can be closing on you from down a corridor you have never seen, and if it is within fourteen tiles it already knows where you are.

08Shooting

RB looses a shot at the nearest thing you can see, if you are carrying something to shoot. The damage model is Angband's, and it is worth stating plainly because it decides what to buy:

(ammunition dice + ammunition bonus + launcher bonus) × the launcher's multiplier

The multiplier is the whole point. A ×4 arbalest turns a good bolt into a great one and a poor bolt into a mediocre one, so the launcher and the ammunition are two separate shopping decisions that multiply rather than add. Seven launchers run from a ×2 short bow to a ×5 Bow of Flame.

Darts and throwing stars need no launcher at all — they are thrown by hand at ×2. That is what gives a character who owns no bow something to do at range, and something to spend early gold on.

A miss still spends the shot. About half of what you loose survives and drops where it landed, so the quiver is something you manage rather than something that only ever drains — and the arrows are lying in the direction you were already going.

The quiver and the launcher have their own equipment slots, so a bow is never competing with the weapon in your hand.

09What you find

An item rolls a base kind weighted by depth, then an enchantment, then a chance at an ego — the same pipeline Moria used. Depth below is the shallowest floor a kind appears on; gold is its base price before enchantment and before the shopkeeper takes a view of your Charisma.

Price answers to utility and rarity together. Each point of enchantment is worth a slice of the item and the slices compound, so a +8 on a Blade of Chaos is a different object from a +8 on a dagger; an ego then multiplies the lot by its own worth, graded against measured power. A curse is worth almost nothing, and a shopkeeper's purse is finite — the best thing you ever find is worth more swung than sold.

The effect strips are drawn in greyscale on purpose, and tinted at the moment of drawing: one set of six frames serves the frost bolt, the fireball and the mana storm, and only the hue changes. Healing is green, blessing gold, detection blue-violet; an attack takes its colour from its power, so arcane, frost, lightning, fire and raw mana each read as themselves.

Four things are worn rather than carried: a weapon, body armour, one ring or amulet, and a light. The gear screen shows what each contributes and what the four add up to, so you can judge a swap without doing the arithmetic yourself. ← there takes a slot off — the only way back to bare hands, and the only way to shed a light before selling it. A cursed item will not come off at all.

10Magic

Sixteen spells across seven shapes. Your class decides which of them you may learn and your level decides when; the failure chance falls as you gain levels and Intelligence, but never below five percent. A failed cast still costs the mana and the turn.

Every attack rolls two dice, so 2d20 is 2–40 with 21 the average. There is no element field: the colour an effect is drawn in comes from that same damage number, in bands — under 12 arcane violet, 12 frost cyan, 16 lightning blue, 28 fire orange, 40 and over raw magenta. Heals are green, buffs gold, detection lilac.

How an effect is drawn

The source tileset shipped a set of monochrome animation strips — a streak growing and shrinking, an expanding shockwave, a burst scattering. Each frame is one 8×8 tile, so an effect is drawn once per tile it covers rather than as a single scaled sprite: a fireball is a bloom of shockwave frames across its blast radius, timed so the outer ring starts late and the whole thing reads as expanding.

11Town

The town is a place, not a screen. It is a walled settlement on the overworld — an irregular wall two courses thick, wandering flagstone streets that punch out through it as gates, and shopfronts standing on the street front. Press A on a shopfront and you are inside it — a room with a counter, shelves and a person behind them. Nothing wild spawns inside the walls — but townsfolk do. Villagers, a farmhand, a sage, a dwarf, a priest, a child and the king wander their own streets and never come at you.

The walled town is the capital and keeps all six traders. Two to four more settlements sit further out without walls — a street with houses either side, an inn, and two or three of the same six traders keeping a door. The trader is one shop with one stock list; the doorway is a place, and the same trader can have a door in two towns. No town puts the same trader behind two of its own doors.

Inside

A shop is a room. The layout is generated from the world seed and which trader it is, so your Armoury is the same shape every time you push its door — a counter across the room with one gap in it, shelving down the walls, torches on the back wall, and the trader standing behind the gap. Walk into them to trade. Walk back out through the door to the street; nothing restocks in between, so a shop cannot be rerolled by stepping out and back in.

The inn is two storeys. Downstairs is the bar: the innkeeper, tables and drinkers. Bump the innkeeper for a hot meal at five gold, which fills the hunger clock. Upstairs is a landing with rooms off it, and each room has a bed — twenty gold to sleep, which restores health, mana and food, and saves your game. Beds appear only up there. The inn on the street is a house with a torch by its door.

Shops restock every time you surface from the dungeon. They buy at a third of what they sell for, which is the usual arrangement.

The Black Market carries things far out of depth at five times the price. It is a money sink and it is meant to be one, but it is also the only place a low-level character will ever see a wand of frost.

Charisma shaves the price a little, which is the only thing Charisma does. Bards and Paladins get a real discount; Berserks do not.

12Money

Finding a great item, selling it, and buying the one you actually wanted is the loop the rest of the game hangs off, so price answers to utility and rarity together rather than to a flat rate.

Each point of enchantment is worth a slice of the item, and the slices compound — so a +8 on a Blade of Chaos is a different object from a +8 on a dagger, which a flat rate per point said it was not. An ego then multiplies the lot by its own worth, and that worth is graded against measured power: "of Attacks" buys a whole extra blow, so it is dearer than a mod with a bigger printed multiplier and no second swing.

A curse is worth almost nothing. This is not flavour — under the first pricing a cursed weapon was worth three times a plain one, and farming curses was the best trade in the game.

A trader's purse is finite, and grows as you go deeper. Uncapped, one legendary find pays for every shop in the game for ever, and the best thing you ever pulled out of a vault becomes a thing you sold rather than a thing you fought with.

Enchantment tops out at +12. Scrolls of enchantment are unlimited and priced off the base item, so without a ceiling a stack of them on a costly weapon is a printing press rather than an upgrade.

Treasure proper — coin, bars, and the cut stones — does nothing at all except be worth money. Denomination runs across the columns and metal down the rows: one coin, a small pile, a large pile, a bar, a nugget, and a stamped sack of gold. Eleven cut gems run from amber to diamond.

13Bestiary

167 kinds, ordered by the depth at which they start appearing. Anything can turn up out of depth — roughly one spawn in ten rolls deeper than the floor it is on, which is where most deaths come from.

14The seventeen

Seventeen floors are guarded. The guardian is not rolled and cannot be missed by bad luck — when you take the stairs to its depth it is there, in the room furthest from where you arrived, awake. Each is a 16×16 sprite rather than the usual 8×8, and each drops one guaranteed item so the fight always pays.

15Dying

Death is permanent and it deletes the save. The tombstone records your class, your level, the deepest floor you reached and your kills, and your score goes to the title screen if it beats the one already there.

score = experience + 250 × deepest floor + 1500 × bosses slain

The inn saves. So does closing the pack, the town screen, or resting. There is one save slot and one character in it; a reload puts you on a freshly generated floor at the depth you left, because that is what the stairs do anyway.

Before you descend
Buy food. Buy a lantern before you buy a better sword — seeing the room first is worth more than hitting harder.
In a corridor
Fight in one. Groups cannot surround you there, and half the shallow bestiary comes in groups.
When it is going badly
The stairs are an escape, not just a way down. So is a scroll of teleport, which is why you should read one early to find out which flavour it is.

Every sprite on this page is cropped live out of the game's own baked sheets, every table is parsed from its source, and the headings are set in the 8×8 CP437 face the game renders with. The engine is Mote.

Every tile, character and effect in this game is from the Simple Roguelike Tileset by Ink_Slime, released CC0. The whole look of Roguemote is their work — 1670 8×8 tiles, catalogued and given jobs, with nothing drawn on top.
