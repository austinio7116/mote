Getting started

CueVR puts a full-size table in the room you are standing in. There is no shot meter and no aiming line: you hold a cue in both hands and play the shot.

You need a Quest 3 or 3S. You also need enough clear floor to walk along one side of a table. The game runs in passthrough, so you will see your own room around the table and can walk around your own furniture.

Your first frame

  1. Put the table somewhere in your room and set its height. See the next section.
  2. On the main menu, work down the three numbered steps — GAME, RULES and VS.
  3. Point at PLAY and pull the trigger.
  4. Pick up the cue with both hands and play.

Finding your way

Almost everything lives on one of two screens: the main menu before a frame, or the pause menu during one. Both open on a sheet of glass in front of you. The glass does not stay where you last left it: every time you open it, it comes up facing you where you are standing now.

The main menu is three numbered steps that set the frame — GAME, RULES and VS — with PLAY beside them. Under the steps is a strip of five panels for how the table looks: TABLE, CLOTH, TIMBER, BALLS and CUE. Down the side are four tiles for everything else: Environment (the table, the scoreboard, the mark round your real table, calibrating a real cue, the lights, the room and a venue of your own), Settings (controls, cue feel, graphics, and sound: the referee, the crowd and voice chat), Records (stats, games played, replays, career) and Workshop (build a table, a ball set, a cue or a challenge).

Point at any of them, pull the trigger, and you get a page of cards or swatches to point at, with the one you are on marked. Nothing steps through a dozen stops one at a time, so reaching a game like COWBOY POOL is one press and then a card.

MENU closes the menu. Holding MENU brings it to where you are standing, from anywhere — including in the middle of a frame.

Setting up the table

One panel, and every way in opens the same one.

The first thing you do is mark the real surface you are playing over: a desk, a dining table, the end of a bed. The game table then stands on it. You do not set the cloth's height separately and you do not turn the cloth separately — where that outline is and how high it is decides all three, so the rail lands over your real edge and your bridge hand comes down on something solid.

Pinning the scoreboard

The scoreboard normally hangs above the far end of the table and moves with it. If you would rather it lived somewhere real — a wall, or a television that is switched off — tap Scoreboard, the second step on the table panel. Grip the board, carry it to where you want it and let go; it stays flat where you left it. Press A when you are done. It keeps that place between sessions, exactly as the table does, until BACK TO AUTO puts it back above the table.

The arena and the rooms have screens of their own. A pinned board is a place in your real room, and they are not your real room — so while you are in one the scoreboard goes where that room keeps it: the arena's screen in the far wall, the pub's television, the club's board at the head of the table, the dining room's gilt frame and the library's frame among the books. Your pinned position is not lost: it comes back the moment you go back to your own room or the dark hall.

Controls

Both controllers hold the cue. The buttons do a small number of jobs, and the two side triggers are the ones worth learning. The side trigger is the grip button on the side of the controller, under your middle finger; some of the panels in the game call it GRIP.

The cue lies between your two controllers, so both hands aim it together: the line of the shot is the line between them. One hand is your bridge hand, where the front of the cue rests. The other is your butt hand, which holds the end and plays the stroke. Which physical controller is which depends on the handedness setting, so everything below names the two hands rather than left and right.

Playing a shot

The cue is a real object lying between your two controllers. The line between your hands is the line of the shot, and how fast you move the butt hand is how hard the ball is hit.

Step by step

  1. Put the tip on the ball. Bring the tip up to the cue ball and line it up with where you want to hit.
  2. Choose your spot on the ball. Raise the tip to hit above centre, lower it to hit below. Move it left or right for side. There is no menu for this: it is simply where the tip is.
  3. Commit. Pull the trigger on your butt hand. This locks the line the cue will travel along, so the shot goes where you aimed it and not where your hands wander to during the delivery.
  4. Stroke through. With the trigger still held, move your butt hand forward through the ball. How fast you move it is how hard the ball is hit: a slow push rolls it gently, a break shot needs a real swing. Release the trigger afterwards.

Ball in hand

When the cue ball is in your hand, it is held just in front of your butt hand's controller (with a real cue, in front of your free hand), and it goes wherever that hand goes. Carry it to where you want it and pull that hand's trigger to put it down. It drops straight onto the cloth and is moved into the area the rules allow, clear of any ball already there, so you cannot put it somewhere illegal.

Putting it down does not start your stroke. With controllers the same trigger holds the cue, so let go of it after placing, then press it again when you are ready to play.

Changed your mind? PICK UP BALL on the pause menu puts it back in your hand, until the stroke is played.

Shots you cannot reach

Bring the shot to you. The sticks move the table at any time, in the middle of a frame included: the left stick slides it, the right stick turns it. Nothing needs to be opened first and nothing is interrupted. For a shot tight against a cushion, hold the butt hand's side trigger and pull that hand back to shorten the cue. If you play over a real table, snap mode can step you into the table instead.

Playing off the rest

For a shot you cannot stretch to, take up the rest with Y on your bridge hand. A brass head goes down on the cloth on the end of an ash stick, and your cue lies in one of its notches: the head is the pivot, and the shot runs through it.

The controller is the butt of the rest, not a pointer. The stick is rigid, so it behaves like one: swing the butt left or right and the head stays where it is — the stick simply pivots about it — while pushing and pulling moves the head along the stick's own line. It is laid a foot short of the cue ball when you pick it up, on the line back to you.

To slide the head sideways, twist the butt and hold. Twist right and it travels right for as long as you hold the twist; come back to where you started and it stops where it got to. It is a throttle rather than a steering wheel, so your wrist never ends up somewhere it has to be unwound from. Both hands moving together carry the head across as well, which is how you shift a rest without lifting it.

The side trigger on the rest hand sends the head further out or draws it back in: hold it and the stick stops being rigid, so your hand's distance from the head becomes the new one. Let go and it is rigid again. The side trigger on your cue hand still sets the cue's own length, as it always does.

The rest is a real length of ash and does not shrink. It is 1.30 m, and it stays 1.30 m. Bring your hand up towards the head and nothing about the rest changes — you are simply holding it further up, and the butt stands out behind your fingers, exactly as it does when you choke up on a cue. Send the head out past that and an extension makes up the difference: the same black barrel the cue screws into its own butt.

Y again cycles the notches — nine of them, at heights from 40 to 96 mm, taking in both faces of the head and both edges. Hold Y to put the rest away. It goes back in the rack by itself after each shot, and remembers which notch you favour.

Feather as much as you like: nothing is a stroke until you hold the trigger, and holding it locks the cue onto its line so the delivery cannot steer itself. The head is frozen from that moment, and it does not drift while you are down on the shot — it sits on cloth, and cloth grips.

With a real cue, your own cue places the rest. Press the rest button and lay your cue down on your real table where you want the bridge: the tip says where and the shaft says which way, and a head is seated on the cloth at that spot. It cannot be a one-to-one mapping — your cue is on your furniture and the head has to end up on the drawn bed — so the tip is dropped onto the cloth and kept on the table, while the line is taken as it lies. The notches still work: press again to step through them, hold to stow. Your free hand is then the cue, playing through the fork. None of the twisting, sliding or stick-length controls apply, because you have put a real object exactly where you want it.

Lagging for the break

Where the rule book has one, a match opens with a lag: both players send a ball up the table and back at the same time, and whoever finishes nearest the baulk cushion has the choice. Yours is the white; your opponent's is the yellow.

A lag must reach the top cushion — anything short of it is no attempt at all, and so is a ball potted, driven off the table, or never struck. A fouled lag loses however close it finished, and if neither is any good, or there is nothing between them, it is played again.

The winner chooses who breaks, which is not the same as winning the break. The choice comes up on its own screen — I'LL BREAK or THEY BREAK — pointed at and pulled like every other list in the game. It happens once, at the top of the match, and every frame after takes its break from what the lag decided.

Pool, UK 8-ball, Chinese 8-ball and the Shoot Out all lag, because their rule books say so. Snooker and English billiards do not: their break is drawn for and then alternates. Practice never lags — there is one seat at the table and nobody to decide the order of play with.

Playing with your own cue

Fix a controller to a real cue and you play with the cue in your hands: your own bridge, your own stroke. First you show the game, once, where the controller sits on your cue. After that the cue on the table goes wherever your real one goes.

What you need. A cue, and something to hold a controller on it: a clamp, a strap or a bracket. Put it on the butt or part way up the shaft, wherever it holds firm. It does not need to be straight or in the middle of the cue. The set-up works that out for you.

Once it is set up, the cue plays the ball when its tip reaches the ball, just like a real one. There is no button to press to strike.

Before you start

  1. Fix the controller on tight. If it can wobble or twist on the cue, the drawn cue wobbles too. If you move it later, you will need to set the cue up again.
  2. Stand where you play, with your cue in your hands.
  3. Set the table height first: Environment → Table, or Table on the pause menu. The set-up is done at that height.

Open the set-up screen

Go to Settings → Cue feel and press CALIBRATE A REAL CUE, at the bottom of the list. The table disappears while you are on this screen, so nothing is in the way, and you see your own room even if you play in the dark room, the arena or one of the other rooms, so you can see the cue you are lining up. Your surroundings come back when you leave the screen.

  1. Say which controller is on your cue. On the second row, press Left controller or Right controller. Do this first: everything else depends on it.
  2. Pick a method on the top row:
    • Quick - assumes 1.45 m takes about ten seconds. Use it for a normal full-length cue, about 57 inches (1.45 m) long.
    • Accurate - two swings takes about a minute. It measures your cue, so use it if your cue is shorter or longer than that, or if Quick leaves the tip in the wrong place.
  3. Press START.

Quick

  1. A grey cue floats in front of you. If it is too high or too low to hold your cue against comfortably, push either thumbstick up or down to move it.
  2. Hold your real cue along the grey one: your tip on its tip, your butt on its butt. Get it as close as you can by eye.
  3. Pull the trigger, the one under your first finger, on either controller.
  4. Press SAVE. Your cue is now switched on.

If the tip looks a little out afterwards, do not start again. Use the fine-tune below.

Accurate

This way you do not line anything up by eye. You rest one end of the cue on something that does not move, and swing the other end around it. The end that is resting stays still while the controller moves round it, so the game can tell exactly where that end is. It does this for the tip and then for the butt, which also tells it how long your cue is.

  1. Press START. The screen says Tip on something solid, then pull the trigger.
  2. Rest the tip on something firm that will not slide: a table top, the seat of a chair, the floor. Hold the butt the way you hold it when you play, not twisted round in your hand: this is how the game learns which way up your cue is. Then pull the trigger. Nothing is measured until you do, so take your time getting settled.
  3. Keep the tip exactly where it is, and slowly swing the butt round it: left and right, and up and down as well, as if you were stirring a big pot with the butt. Two bars on the screen, ACROSS and UP AND DOWN, fill up as you go. Keep going until both are full.
  4. A small marker shows where the game thinks your tip is. If it sits on your tip and stays there while you swing, it is right. The screen says Tip found - trigger to go on. Pull the trigger.
  5. Now turn the cue round. Rest the butt on something firm, pull the trigger, and swing the tip round it in the same way until both bars are full.
  6. The screen says Measured - trigger to finish. Pull the trigger. It then shows the length it measured, for example 1.45 m of cue. If that is about the length of your cue, it worked. If it is clearly wrong, 0.4 m say, the resting end moved: press START AGAIN.
  7. Press SAVE. Your cue is now switched on.
Keep the resting end still. If it slides even a little while you swing, the measurement is spoiled. Rest it on something grippy, like a rubber mat or a towel on the floor, and swing slowly.

Fine-tuning it with the side trigger

Neither method is perfect first time. This puts it right, and you can do it whenever you like, even at the table in the middle of a frame. The side trigger is the grip button on the side of the controller, under your middle finger.

  1. Lay your real cue on something flat, like a table or a desk, so it is still and you can see it clearly against the drawn cue.
  2. Tap the side trigger on the cue's controller, then press it again and hold it. The drawn cue freezes in the air and stops following you. The tap has to come first: your hand rests on that button all through a game, and the tap tells the game you mean it.
  3. Keep holding, and slide your real cue until it lies exactly under the drawn one: tip under tip, shaft under shaft.
  4. If CUE TURNS WITH YOUR WRIST is on, also roll your real cue until its markings, the points and the badge, line up with the drawn cue's.
  5. Let go of the side trigger. The drawn cue now follows your real cue from where you put it. It saves itself; there is nothing else to press.

Do it as often as you like. Each time replaces the last, so small errors cannot build up.

Where it works. On the set-up screen, and at the table during a frame, on your turn or your opponent's. It does not work in the menus, where the side trigger does other jobs.

One stick has to do everything

A controller clamped to a cue is on the end of a metre and a half of timber. The game assumes you cannot reach it, so with a real cue set up, everything you need is on the hand that is free.

Nothing here is different for the ordinary two-controller cue — those controls are exactly as they always were. It only changes once a cue is calibrated and CUE is set to REAL CUE.

But it is on both hands anyway. Every one of these is mirrored onto the cue controller as well, in case your clamp does leave it usable. If it does, nothing is taken away from you. Set TABLE MOVEMENT to ONE STICK, EITHER and both hands do the same job.
Which hand is which comes from your cue, not from the handedness setting. Once a cue is calibrated, the game takes the controller you clamped it to as the answer: that hand is the cue hand and the other is your free hand. The rest, the overhead view, the scoreboard and the mark all follow it. Nothing to set — but it is why picking the other controller on the set-up screen changes more than the calibration.

One stick cannot do two things at once, so the side trigger switches what it is doing. It is the same gesture on all three screens that need it, and the panel always says which job the stick is on — you never have to remember.

Marking your real table

This is the first step of the table panel, and the one worth doing properly. If the cloth is drawn over a real piece of furniture you can lean on it, rest a bridge hand on its edge and walk into it safely — and the game table stands on whatever you mark, so marking it well is how the cloth ends up at the right height and the right way round.

Stand at the edge of your desk, point along it and hold the trigger: the outline comes to your hand, at your hand's height, running the way you are pointing. Let go and it stays. From there the left stick slides it, the right stick sizes it, grip with the right stick turns and raises it, and you can grip any corner and drag it onto your real corner. RESET THE OUTLINE puts it back under the table if you lose it.

Or use both hands: hold both triggers and the line between them is the near edge, so the width comes from your reach along with the angle and the height. A hand on each corner of your desk sets the whole thing in one go.

The bright edge is the side you play from; amber means you are standing at a different one and should rotate the outline round to you. Show it while you play — Never, Always, or On press — from the same panel.

Snap to the edges

Once the mark is in, the TABLE MOVEMENT row has a setting built around it: SNAP TO THE EDGES. Stand at your real table's edge and the virtual table squares itself to the edge you marked — the outer wood of the frame lands exactly on your real edge, so when you rest your bridge hand on the rail it is resting on real timber.

  • The slide stick slides the table along that edge, and nothing else. There is no forward or back, so the table can never drift off your real edge.
  • The turn stick snap-turns: each push brings the next side of the table round to you, square on your real edge again. You can work all the way round the table without it ever leaving your furniture.
  • Or point at the side you want. POINT AT THE EDGE, on the SNAP TURN row underneath, changes what the turn stick means: instead of stepping round one edge at a time, the stick becomes a picture of the table seen from where you stand. The edge under your hands is six o'clock, so pushing away brings the far side round in one press, and pushing left or right takes you to that side. On a hexagon or an octagon it still works, because the stick is read as an angle and rounded to whichever edge is nearest it.
  • Hold the stick forward for a second and you step into the table, for the shots no edge can reach. Inside, you have full movement and 90° turns about the cue ball; the table goes back to your edge by itself after the shot, or when a new frame starts.
The far edge is what moves when you change the depth. A table made deeper or shallower used to grow at both ends, which pushed the near cushion off the real edge you had just squared it to — the one thing this mode exists to hold still. It now extends away from you instead, so the edge under your hands stays exactly where it is and the far end goes out to meet the size you asked for.
A longer cue, when you want one. With the ordinary two-controller cue, sliding your grip hand off the back of the butt fits an extension: your hand stays where it is, the tip goes further out, and 200 mm of timber appears behind your hand. Slide back on and it comes off again.

Snooker

12FT TABLE · 15 REDS · 6 COLOURS

Pot a red, then a colour, then another red, and so on. Each red you pot stays down; each colour goes back on its spot until the reds are finished. When the last red and the colour after it are gone, you clear the colours in order from yellow to black.

Values. Red 1, yellow 2, green 3, brown 4, blue 5, pink 6, black 7.

Nominating. When you are on a colour you have to say which one, and the game takes the colour you aim at.

Fouls are hitting nothing, hitting the wrong ball first, potting the cue ball, jumping the cue ball over a ball, and driving any ball off the table. The penalty is four points, or the value of the ball you were on, the ball you hit first, or the highest ball you potted — whichever of those is greatest. The points go to your opponent.

No cushion requirement. Unlike the pool games, a shot that hits the ball on and sends nothing to a cushion and pots nothing is perfectly legal — that is what a safety often is.

After a foul your opponent plays from where the balls are, or from in hand if the cue ball was potted. If the foul leaves them unable to hit the ball on, they get a free ball: they may nominate any ball as the one they are on, and it scores what the real ball on is worth.

Miss. Failing to hit the ball on is always a foul, and the referee judges whether it is also a miss — whether your attempt was good enough — at the standard set on the RULES row (see Rules and the referee). When a miss is called your opponent may make you play the shot again from exactly where it was. Failing to escape a snooker counts — that is the most common miss there is. The one exemption is when you can no longer catch up on the balls left on the table: a player needing snookers is not called for a miss, so a hopeless frame cannot be dragged out by replays.

Three misses in a row lose the frame, but only genuine ones: a miss played from a snooker does not count towards it.

The end. Once the black is potted the frame is over. If the scores are level, the black is respotted, the player who did not pot it plays first from in hand, and the next score wins.

WPA rules. The table is open after the break — whatever you pot first decides whether you are solids or stripes — and once your group is clear you are on the 8.

Fouls are potting the cue ball, hitting nothing, hitting the other group first, hitting the 8 first while the table is open or before your group is clear, hitting anything other than the 8 once you are on it, driving a ball off the table, and — as WPA rules require — playing a shot that neither pots a ball nor sends any ball to a cushion.

Ball in hand. Any foul gives your opponent the cue ball anywhere on the table, not just behind a line. It is a heavy penalty.

9-ball

9FT TABLE · 9 BALLS · DIAMOND RACK

You must always hit the lowest numbered ball on the table first, but any ball may then go in. Pot the 9 legally and you win at once — including straight off the break, and including off a combination.

Fouls are potting the cue ball, hitting nothing, hitting anything other than the lowest ball first, failing to pot a ball or send any ball to a cushion after the contact, and driving a ball off the table.

Ball in hand anywhere on the table for the opponent, as at US 8-ball. If the 9 was potted on a foul it goes back on its spot. Three fouls in a row loses the frame.

The push-out. The player who takes the first shot after the break may declare a push-out: they may hit the cue ball anywhere they like, with no need to hit the lowest ball or reach a cushion, and the only foul is potting the cue ball. 10-ball

9FT TABLE · 10 BALLS · TRIANGLE RACK

The same bed and the same rotation game as 9-ball: lowest ball first, any ball may then go in, three fouls in a row loses the frame, and the money ball ends it. The rack is what changes — ten balls out of a triangle instead of nine out of a diamond. A diamond opens up off almost any break and a triangle does not, so the table stays cluttered and the lowest ball is more often tied up.

The rack puts the 1 on the foot spot at the apex, the 10 in the middle — the centre of the third row — and the 2 and 3 on the two back corners, rows of one, two, three and four.

Chinese 8-ball

Straight pool

9FT TABLE · 15 BALLS · 50 UP

14.1 continuous, the old scoring game. There is no ball you must hit, no group and no order: every ball on the table is worth one point, whichever ball it is. You pick one, say it is going down, and if it does you score and carry on. First to 50 wins the frame — the real game is played to hundreds, this one is cut to a frame that fits a session.

Calling a ball. The ball you are aiming at is the ball you have called; there is no menu for it and nothing to press. Whatever your cue is pointing at when you strike is what you have declared. Aim at nothing in particular and you have called nothing, which makes the stroke a safety.

Scoring. Pot the called ball and it scores one, and so does every other ball that happened to go down on the same stroke — the accidental extra is a real part of the game. If the call is not made, nothing on that stroke counts and every ball that went down comes straight back up onto the long string behind the foot spot.

In practice it never runs out. With the opponent set to FREE PRACTICE, when one ball is left the other fourteen are racked again, as in the real game, so you can keep going.

Your visit. Keep making your calls and you stay at the table. Miss one and your opponent is in, and the board says MISSED CALL — or SAFETY, if you called nothing at all.

The pocket is optional. Real 14.1 calls ball and pocket, and the RULES row carries both readings. On CALL THE BALL, the default, you name only the ball and it may go down any pocket, so a called ball that drops in a pocket you never intended still scores. On CALL BALL AND POCKET it has to be the pocket you named as well, and that is the only setting on this game that lights the pocket lamps — how you say a call is under Rules and the referee.

Fouls are potting the cue ball, hitting nothing, driving a ball off the table, and a stroke that neither pots a ball nor sends any ball to a cushion. A foul is minus one, and the opening break is the one stroke that costs minus two — the break has to send two object balls to a cushion or pot a called ball. Three fouls in a row costs fifteen more and the whole table is racked again with the offender breaking it.

The rerack, which is what continuous means. You never clear all fifteen. When one ball is left the other fourteen go back as a triangle with the apex space empty; the break ball and the cue ball stay exactly where they lie, and that break ball is what you use to open the new rack. Your visit is not interrupted — play carries straight on, which is why the fifteenth stays where it lies while the other fourteen are re-racked. Clear the table outright and all fifteen go back and you break from in hand.

One visit, start to finish. Fourteen balls are still on and you are down on the 12 near the side pocket. You aim at it and strike; it drops, and the 5 it clipped on the way in drops too — two points, and you are still at the table. You line up the 9 next, hit it too thin, and it stays out: MISSED CALL, and your opponent is in. Under CALL THE BALL that is the whole of it. Under CALL BALL AND POCKET you would have had to name the pocket the 12 was going in as well, and a 12 that fell anywhere else would have come straight back up.

Russian pyramid

12FT TABLE · 67 MM BALLS · 15 IVORIES · 8 WINS

Any of the fifteen is on, in any order, and each one you pot scores one. Eight wins: more than half, so it cannot be caught.

Pockets barely wider than the ball. A corner is under five millimetres wider than the ball and a middle fourteen (81 mm, as a tournament table's), on a ball 67 mm across, both measured at the narrowest point. That leaves a couple of millimetres either side of a straight corner pot. The middles are the wider of the two, the opposite of every other table here, and the jaws are cut as a straight mitre rather than the English curve. The point of each jaw is rounded as the federation's rules ask: 5 mm at the corners and 11 mm at the middles, and the cushion's nose stands 42 mm above the cloth.

Club tables. CLUB 12 FT and CLUB 7 FT, in the table picker, have the more forgiving pockets clubs keep for beginners and casual play: 74 mm corners and 83 mm middles on the 12 ft.

The house, not a D. You play from the дом: the whole width of the table behind the baulk line rather than an arc. A pyramid table has no D, so none is drawn.

Three sets of rules, on the card's RULES row: COMBINED (the default), CLASSIC and FREE PYRAMID. All three are first to eight, from the house.

COMBINED is the Russian federation's own discipline, and the one most played. The coloured ball is always the cue ball, and nothing is called: every ball potted on a fair stroke scores one.

  • Pot the cue ball off a white and it counts too: you take any ball you like off the table for a point (look or point at it and pull the trigger), and play on from in hand in the house. From the house, the first ball you hit must lie outside it. If every ball is in the house, you play from the other end instead.
  • The break has to open the pack: a ball potted, or three balls to a cushion, or two to a cushion and one across the centre line. If it does not, your opponent takes a ball for a point and then chooses: play on, make you play on, re-rack and break, or re-rack and make you break again.
  • With nothing potted, a stroke is only fair if some ball, after the first contact, comes off a cushion and then touches another cushion, or drives a ball to another cushion, or crosses the centre line; or crosses the centre line and then reaches a cushion, or drives a ball to one. One cushion on its own is not enough.
  • A foul — hitting nothing, a stroke that does not do enough, a ball off the table, a first ball struck in the house from hand — gives your opponent any ball off the table for a point. If the cue ball went in or off, they play from the house. Anything a foul potted, and any ball driven off the table, goes back on the spot.

CLASSIC is the game as it was first shipped. Fouls are hitting nothing, potting the cue ball, potting nothing and reaching no cushion, and driving a ball off the table. The penalty is the sting of the game: one of your own potted balls goes back on the table for your opponent to shoot at, and they play from in hand in the house. A frame can go backwards.

Fifteen plain ivories with a small black numeral on both poles, and a red cue ball. With fifteen identical balls, that colour is the only thing telling you which one you are about to strike.

Your opponent plays it as the game it is. On a pocket this size what decides a pot is not how thin the cut is but the angle the object ball ARRIVES at: much past twenty degrees off the middle of the hole it meets a jaw however well it was struck, which is why nothing is ever potted along a cushion here. The computer looks for balls already sitting on a line into a pocket rather than for balls it can cut in, and it will not give up such a line to buy itself a better angle on the next one — which is the trade every other game on the row is built around, and the wrong trade in this one.

FREE PYRAMID: the break is played from the house as usual; after that any ball can be your cue ball — whichever one your cue is lined up on is the one you strike. Every ball you pot counts, including the one you struck once it has hit another ball, and first to eight wins. A foul puts back whatever it potted, and your opponent then takes any ball they like off the table and scores it, naming it the way you name a ball (your CALL setting in the controls), and plays on from where the balls lie. A ball driven off the table goes back on the spot, and it is only a foul if it was the ball you struck.

English billiards

12FT TABLE · THREE BALLS · 50 TO 200 UP, OR A CLOCK

Two whites and a red on the full-size snooker table. There are no pockets to clear and no order to keep: you score by cannons, pots and in-offs.

They all count on the same stroke, so a cannon that pots both balls is seven. An in-off played with a cannon is priced by the ball you struck first — three if that was the red, two if it was the white.

An in-off leaves you in hand and you carry on. A legal stroke that scores nothing ends your turn and the table is played as it lies.

The red always comes back — on the Spot, or the Pyramid Spot if that is occupied, or the Centre Spot if both are. Pot the red over and over with nothing else in the stroke and it goes Spot, Spot, Centre in sequence. A potted white waits for its owner: it is off the table until their turn, and they then play from in hand.

Caps — 75 consecutive cannons and 15 consecutive hazard strokes. Exceeding either is a foul.

Every foul is two to your opponent and never more than two however many rules one stroke broke; the points already made in the break stand. A coup — in hand, cue ball pocketed, nothing struck — is a foul and not an in-off.

What the game is played to is set on the RULES row: 50, 75, 100, 150 or 200 up, or a clock of 5, 10, 15 or 30 minutes. On a clock the referee calls TIME, the stroke already played is allowed to finish and score, and the higher score wins; level scores are a draw.

Double baulk. Leave both object balls behind the baulk line while your opponent is in hand and they cannot play straight at either one: from the D the cue ball has to be played out of baulk, and no part of a ball that is in baulk may be struck directly. Breaking that is a foul. If they play out properly and simply fail to hit anything, that is a miss — still two points, but not a foul.

From in hand, always. The same rule applies every time you play from the D, not only on the break: after an in-off, after your opponent pots your ball, after a foul. To reach a ball in baulk you must come off a cushion out of baulk first; coming back off the baulk cushion does not count. The computer plays by it too.

Potting your opponent's ball scores two and you play on from where your own ball lies.

At every change of turn the two whites swap over, so the ball in front of you is always your own.

Bar billiards

142 × 79 CM · NINE HOLES IN THE BED · NO RAIL POCKETS · A RED AND SEVEN WHITES

The odd one out in every way. Four unbroken cushions and no pockets on the rails at all — instead there are nine holes bored through the bed, worth 10 to 200, and three wooden skittles standing among them. Eight balls: one red and seven whites, all the same size. There is no cue ball to scratch. Every white on the table is one, you strike whichever the shot wants, and a white down a hole is a score.

The red scores double, so the red in the 200 is 400.

Where the holes and skittles are

The four holes nearest you are a diamond: the 200 at the front of it with the black skittle standing right in front of that, the two 50s at its sides, and the 100 at its back between the two white skittles. Across the far end is a row of five: 30-20-10-20-30.

Every stroke is played from the D at the near end, so you are always shooting away from the 200. Reaching it means bringing a ball back down the table off a cushion or off another ball, and past a skittle you must not knock over. The easy scoring is the row of five at the far end.

The 200, the 100 and the 10 all sit on the centre line, so a ball sent straight up the middle drops into the first of them it reaches — which is why the 100 and the 10 have to be played on an angle.

A ball has to be rolled in, not driven. Rolled in gently it drops; hit hard it skips the far lip and carries on up the table. The holes are real holes with the cloth turned over the edge and a shaft below, so this is the same free fall a real board gives you.

Playing it
Where you play from The D at the base, every stroke — a half-disc of 40 mm radius round the break spot. The opening stroke is played from the spot itself, and the last-ball stroke from the middle of the D.
Which ball you get One out of the trough while the rack still holds one. When the rack is empty you take the ball on the table furthest from the top cushion.
Your break Runs on as long as you keep potting. A stroke that pots nothing ends it, and so does any foul.
Fouls cost your break, not points Knocking over a white skittle; hitting nothing; sending a ball off the table or back over the baulk lines; and potting from the break position four times running (you get a "LEAVE ONE UP" warning after the third).
The black skittle Knock it over and you lose your entire score. If a white went over as well, whichever fell first decides which penalty applies.
The end A 17-minute clock, not a rack running out — a coin buys fifteen to twenty minutes on a real one. When it expires the bar drops: potted balls stop coming back out of the trough and play carries on until the balls are swallowed.
The last ball With one ball left, the two white skittles are moved into the 50 holes and the stroke is played from the middle of the D. It only counts if the ball goes into the 100 or the 200 off one side cushion, and touching a white skittle voids it. Players take turns until it goes down — or the black falls.

Bumper pool

142 × 79 CM · TWO CUPS · TWELVE BUMPERS · FIVE BALLS EACH

A small table with no pockets on the rails. There are two cups sunk in the cloth, one at each end on the centre line, and twelve bumpers — rubber-collared posts standing at cushion height — eight of them in a cross across the middle and two guarding each cup.

Five red and five white, and no cue ball. The reds are one player's and the whites are the other's; you strike your own balls, and the one you are pointing at is the one you will play. Your five start at the far end from the cup you are shooting into, so the opening stroke is a bank off a side cushion and not a roll down the table.

One ball of each five is marked, and it has to go down first. Until it does, another of your balls dropping in your cup counts for nothing and comes straight back out onto its spot.

Your own ball in your own cup scores, and you carry on. Your own ball in your opponent's cup is a forfeit: they lift two of theirs and count them, taken at the start of their turn. Put one of their balls down their cup and it counts for them. First to get all five down takes it.

The bumpers turn a ball the way a cushion does — they are rigid posts with a rubber collar, and a ball comes off one with rather less pace than it went in. Most of the table is played around them rather than through them, and the computer plays it the same way: it looks for a line to its cup, and when the posts have taken every straight one away it banks.

A visit. Start on the ball on, or on any other ball — but any other ball has to go in, or it costs you 7. Billiards golf

7FT TABLE · 18 HOLES · PAR 73

Eighteen holes on the pub table, off the old Billiard & Golf scoreboard. Each hole is a fixed arrangement of two, three or four reds inside the rack triangle, with a par of one more than the number of balls. Strokes are the score and fewer is better.

There are no fouls at all. No ball you must hit, no cushion requirement, no turn to lose. Missing everything simply costs you a stroke. Potting the cue ball costs one extra — so that stroke costs two — and the white goes back to the middle of the baulk line with the reds left exactly where they lie. Every other stroke is played from where the ball is.

The scoreboard carries your card as you play. Each player's holes so far, their total, and how they stand against the par of the holes they have played — the number a golfer actually wants, without opening anything.

A hole ends when the last red goes down, or at eight strokes, whichever comes first. The board tells you where you are in golfer's language: "HOLE 4 — 2 FOR PAR", then "THIS FOR PAR", then "THIS FOR BOGEY".

Two players play the same hole from identical racks. The lower score takes the honour and leads off the next hole; a tie leaves the honour where it was. Low total wins the round. The RULES row chooses eighteen holes, the front nine (par 38) or the back nine (par 35).

Or play it as matchplay, which is the same holes scored as a different game. The RULES row has all three rounds a second time — MATCHPLAY 18, MATCHPLAY FRONT 9 and MATCHPLAY BACK 9 — and in those, each hole is won, lost or halved on its own and the totals are thrown away. The board stops talking about par and says where the match is: ALL SQUARE, or who is how many holes up.

And you pick up a hole you cannot tie. Playing second at a hole, once you have taken as many strokes as your opponent needed and the reds are not gone, nothing you do can halve it — so the visit ends there rather than making you play out a hole that is already lost. The card shows a dash where the score would have been, which is what a paper matchplay card carries, and the hole counts as lost exactly as if you had played it out.

It changes what a bad hole costs. In strokeplay a fourteen follows you round for the rest of the round; in matchplay it loses that hole and nothing else, and you can be all square on the next tee having been beaten by nine strokes on the last one. It also ends when it is decided rather than when the holes run out — more holes up than there are holes left and the match is over, which the board calls in golfer's shorthand: 3 AND 2.

Paul

6FT HOME TABLE · 42 MM BALLS · SCATTERED AT RANDOM · 54 MM POCKETS

There is no rack. The whole snooker set — fifteen reds, six colours and the white — goes onto the cloth at random, differently every game, so there is no opening to learn and nothing to break. The white lies wherever it landed.

The break hits nothing. The opening stroke must touch no ball and no cushion. Touch either, or pot anything, and it is a foul worth two shots to your opponent.

Then alternate visits, and pot whatever you can reach — there is no ball on and no order. A red is one, a colour is two, the black is four, and everything potted stays down: nothing is ever spotted. A miss is not a foul; it simply ends your visit. An in-off is a foul, worth two shots to your opponent, and the ball it potted scores nothing. Two shots in hand are spent by potting, and after a scratch the white goes in the D, which is the only thing the D is for in this game.

You win by leading by more than is left. Twenty-nine points go on the table at the start, and the frame ends the moment one player is further ahead than the remaining balls can make up. If the table empties with the scores level, the black goes back on its spot and you play for it.

One pocket

9FT TABLE · 15 BALLS · 8 UP · US 8-BALL'S RULES ROW

The most tactical game on a pool table, and unlike anything else here. You own one of the two far corner pockets and your opponent owns the other. A ball is worth something to whoever owns the hole it went down and to nobody else, and eight of the fifteen wins it.

The breaker chooses, after the break. Not before — you pick your pocket once you have seen what the break left, which is how the game is played and half of what makes the opening stroke worth thinking about. The board asks you and the table waits; the other corner is then your opponent's. Against the computer it chooses for itself, and quickly.

Which pocket is yours is lit on the table — yours steady green, your opponent's steady red, both lit for the whole frame. The other four pockets stay dark, which is exactly their status: a ball down one of those is spotted and your visit is over.

A ball in your opponent's pocket scores for them, and there is no way to refuse it. A ball down is down.

A foul costs a ball, not just the turn. One of your scored balls comes back out on to the table, and if you have none yet you owe one, taken from the first ball you do score. That is what makes the game the famous crawl it is: two players nudging balls a millimetre at a time, because a foul is more expensive than a missed chance.

Rotation, and the games on its board

9FT TABLE · 15 BALLS · 61 UP · US 8-BALL'S RULES ROW

The oldest of the rotation family and the reason the family has that name. The lowest ball on the table is always the one you must hit first, and a ball is worth its own number. The numbers come to 120, so 61 wins — and the frame can be over with balls still up, which is the whole shape of it. The 15 alone is a quarter of what you need, so a player who took the low balls cheaply can still lose to one who took the high ones.

The rack is rotation's own: the 1 at the apex nearest you, the 2 and 3 in the far corners, the 15 buried in the middle. The ball you are obliged to hit is the closest one, the next two lowest are as far away as the table allows, and the biggest score cannot be had cheaply.

ROTATION 61The classic game. A foul puts the incoming player behind the head string, which is a real penalty: you may not shoot at anything in front of the line, so a pack at the far end has to be reached the long way.
FILIPINO ROTATIONThe same board, two rules changed, and they are the two that decide how it feels. Bank pool, Honolulu and speed pool

9FT TABLE · 15 BALLS · US 8-BALL'S RULES ROW

Three more games on the same bed, each built on refusing you one ordinary thing.

BANK POOLCall the ball and the pocket, and bank it in. Only the called ball, banked into the called pocket, scores — one ball a stroke. Anything else that drops goes back on the table, and that is not a foul, so if your called ball went in you keep the table. The break is not called, and what it banks in counts.

What a bank is. The ball must come off a rail that does not run into the pocket it drops in. A rail is the cushion between two pockets, jaws included, so off the far side of the table is a bank, and so is off the jaw of a different pocket. A ball that runs along one of the pocket's own rails, or clips that pocket's jaw on the way in, has not been banked. First to 8, and a foul costs a ball as it does at one pocket.
HONOLULUA straight-in pot scores nothing. The ball has to arrive the hard way — off a bank, off a kick (the cue ball finding a cushion before it reaches a ball), or through another ball as a combination or carom. A bank is judged as it is at bank pool: off a rail that does not run into the pocket. The one shot every other game on this table is built around is the only one this game does not have. A straight pot is not a foul: it goes back on the table, and if you scored one properly on the same stroke you keep the table. First to 8.
SPEED POOLNot a frame at all. You each clear a full rack and the clock is the score — lowest time wins. There is no turn to take from anybody and nothing to defend: any ball, any pocket, as fast as you can get to the next one. Potting the cue ball is not a foul — it comes back in your hand and the clock keeps running, which costs you more than a lost turn would.

Bowlliards

9FT TABLE · 10 BALLS · ITS OWN CARD

Pool kept on a ten-pin bowling card, and the only game here besides golf that you play against a card rather than against the person opposite. Ten balls, ten frames, and a maximum of 300.

A frame is two innings to clear the rack. Clear all ten in the first and it is a strike; clear them across both and it is a spare; anything else scores the balls you potted. The break is free — nothing on it counts, everything goes back up, and a scratch costs nothing — and then you call ball and pocket on every stroke.

The scoring is a real bowling card, which means a frame cannot be totalled when you finish it. A strike is worth ten and the next two innings; a spare ten and the next one. So the box stays blank until the balls that score it have been thrown, exactly as on paper, and the tenth frame earns two extra innings for a strike and one for a spare.

The board shows the five frames you are in, marked the way a bowler marks them: X a strike, / a spare, - a miss. MENU, then SCORECARD, for all ten.

There is nothing to play safe for — you are clearing your own rack and the table changes hands when your inning ends, not before — so the machine never plays safe at it either. It takes every pot however thin and chooses between them by where the cue ball finishes, which is what clearing ten actually asks of you.

The RULES row offers CANADIAN house rules as well as the standard game. Under those you do not call anything — no ball, no pocket, pot what you can. The break is a scoring stroke: whatever goes down on it stays down and counts towards your first inning. You play the white from wherever it finishes rather than taking it in hand, unless you potted it — a scratch on the break costs you the position and nothing else. And failing to reach a cushion is still a foul, but the incoming player takes the table as it lies instead of with the ball in hand.

Cribbage pool

9FT TABLE · 15 BALLS · US 8-BALL'S RULES ROW

The numbers on the balls are worth nothing on their own. What scores is a cribbage — two balls totalling fifteen, potted one after the other inside a single inning. There are exactly seven of them in a rack: 1+14, 2+13, 3+12, 4+11, 5+10, 6+9 and 7+8. Five cribbages takes the game, and the rack is not played out once somebody has them.

In succession is the whole game. Pot the 4 and you are on a cribbage: the next stroke must pot the 11, there is no option to leave it, and failing is a foul that spots the unpaired ball and ends your inning. Pot two openers on one stroke and you owe both — take them in whichever order you like, but keep going until the list is empty.

The 15 pairs with nothing and can start no cribbage. Potted early it is spotted and that is not a foul; once every other ball is down it becomes a cribbage on its own, and it decides a game that is level.

The board carries all seven fifteens. A pair struck through has gone, a pair lit is open, and the ball you MUST POT is named beside them — which matters, because while you owe one it is the only legal shot on the table.

Rules and the referee

The rules are enforced for you, and called out loud. You do not have to keep score or remember what is on.

The top line says which game this is, and which rule set it is being played under — UK 8-BALL and BLACKBALL, SNOOKER and MISS: PRO, BILLIARDS GOLF and 18 HOLES - MATCHPLAY. It used to say POOL, which is true of thirty of the thirty-two games, and the rule set appeared nowhere once you had left the menu.

At snooker that includes reds and colours in the right order, colours going back on their spots, free balls after a foul, called misses, and the final clearance from yellow to black. At the pool games it includes your group, the two-shot carry at UK 8-ball, and the push-out at 9-ball. Elsewhere it includes cannons and in-offs at English billiards, the called ball at straight pool, the cushions counted before the second ball at carom, and the skittles and the clock at bar billiards.

The rules of the game you are playing

The referee is exact and it is silent: it will foul you for something you did not know was a foul, and until now the only place to look it up was this page, on a screen you are not wearing. So every game carries its own short rule book. Press MENU and choose RULES OF THIS GAME, and there it is — what you are trying to do, how you score, what a foul costs and how the frame ends. Snooker, one pocket and bank pool run past a page: hold the trigger on it and drag to scroll. The thumbstick still moves the table.

It is the rules of the frame in front of you, not of the family it belongs to. The variant is named under the title and only its rules are printed: choose PUB and it tells you about two shots, choose BLACKBALL and it tells you about the free shot, the jump foul and the sixty-second clock. It never lists four rule sets and leaves you to work out which one you are on.

Hold X at any point and the board is replaced by a live photograph of the table from above, for as long as you hold it.

The ball you cued is the ball you struck. Nothing lifts or steers your cue onto the white for you, so if another ball is standing on the line, your tip reaches that one first and that is the ball you have played. It is a foul, charged in each game's own currency: four away at snooker, two at billiards, ball in hand at 9-ball and pyramid, minus two at straight pool, the turn at UK 8-ball, your break at bar billiards. Anything the wrong ball knocked in stays potted.
A cushion can snooker you. On a rectangle only balls can get in the way. On an L-shaped or many-sided bed you can be laid behind the elbow, so anything the cue ball cannot pass through counts as a snooker.

When your opponent fouls

You are asked what you want to do. You can play on from where the balls have finished, or make your opponent play the shot again. If a miss was called and the table can be put back as it was, that is offered too. The referee announces what you chose.

Foul and a miss

In the snooker games, failing to hit the ball on is always a foul — but whether it is also called a miss depends on the effort you made, exactly as a real referee judges it.

Where to set it. The RULES row on the snooker games. It has three strictnesses, and it can be turned off, in which case there is no judgement at all and every failure to hit the ball on is a miss, as it always was before the rule existed.

MISS: AMATEURA genuine try is enough — within about three ball widths, with most of the pace to get there.
MISS: CLUBThe default. Within a ball and a half, at nearly full pace.
MISS: PROAs on television: almost every failure is called. Within about a third of a ball, and the cue ball must have had the pace to pass the ball on.

How the referee decides. He judges the stroke against the ball you came nearest to, not against every legal ball on the table — a stroke aimed at one red is not marked down for the fourteen it ignored. Two things then settle it:

How close you cameMeasured in ball widths — roughly three balls at AMATEUR, a ball and a half at CLUB, a third of a ball at PRO.
Whether it was a real attemptHow far the cue ball travelled against how far it had to go. A dribble that stops short is a miss however well aimed it was.

Two allowances are made on top of that, because a referee makes them. You get three times the room when you were snookered — an escape off three cushions that finishes a ball's width away is a good attempt by anybody's reckoning — and more room the longer the shot, so nobody is called for drift on a twelve-foot roll that the same drift on a tap from a foot away would be called for. Three called misses in a row lose the frame — but only ones you were not snookered for: an attempt the referee accepted, or a miss played from a snooker, does not count towards the three, and a single accepted attempt clears the tally.

The same row has two more stops, SHOOTOUT and THE 900. They replace the miss rule rather than sitting beside it: each is a different frame altogether, played against a clock — see The games.

Calling a pocket

To call a shot is to say, before you play it, which ball you are potting and where it is going. Four games can ask for it.

Most of the time you say nothing at all. When the pot in front of you is the obvious one, the game calls it for you and you simply play the shot. That is how it behaves already; it is not a setting and there is nothing to switch on. You only ever have to do anything on the strokes that are genuinely ambiguous — which is what stops a call-shot game turning into paperwork.

Where you set it is the RULES row on the main menu, and each game words it its own way:

Straight poolCALL THE BALL, the default, wants the ball only, and it may go down any pocket. CALL BALL AND POCKET wants both — nothing counts unless it was declared first, and that is the real game.
10-ballCALL SHOT (WPA). Every stroke is called; the break is not.
US 8-ballCALL THE BLACK asks only for the last one, which is how most people play. CALL SHOT (WPA) asks for every stroke, which is how the rule book has it.
Chinese 8-ballThe same two, with CALL SHOT (CBSA) for the tournament reading.

What the lamps mean. There is one behind each pocket:

Flashing greenThe game's own call. It has picked this pocket for you; play the shot and it stands.
Solid greenA pocket you declared.
RedA call is owed on this stroke, and this is not the pocket it is on.
Amber, filling upThe pocket you are looking at now, filling as the dwell runs. Let it fill and it is called.
GreyNothing needs calling this stroke.

The called ball gets a chalk ring on the cloth as well, so one glance tells you both halves of what is booked. Nothing is ever called on the break, and the scoreboard says NO CALL NEEDED so you are not left wondering.

Overruling the game's choice — or making the call yourself when the shot is ambiguous — is the CALL A POCKET row on CONTROLS, and there are three ways to do it:

LOOK + TRIGGERHold the trigger on your free hand, look at a ball or a pocket, and release to call it.
LOOK, STOOD UPLook and hold, no trigger. It only listens while you are stood up off the shot, so looking around while you are down on the ball changes nothing.
POINT + TRIGGERA laser from your free hand — or from the cue itself, if you have a real cue attached.

The computer players call their pockets too, from the plan they actually made, and you see their call light up while their shot is still rolling.

How they play

They aim at the real opening the cushion noses leave rather than at a line through the middle of the pocket, and on a cut they err towards the far jaw, which is the safer side to be wrong on. Given ball in hand they work back from the shots the position offers, and they always leave themselves able to reach the ball on.

They play the newer games too. English billiards has its own planner, since a cannon is the commonest scoring stroke there and it pockets nothing, while an in-off scores here and fouls everywhere else. Bar billiards has one as well: it plays in-offs, plants, contacts at five thicknesses and one-cushion shots around a skittle, and it will not risk the black skittle.

They break off at snooker the way a snooker player does. Thin on the outside red with a touch of side and a little top, off the top cushion, back down between the pink and the blue and into baulk — on any of the three snooker table sizes. How close they get to it is their skill: the stronger players land it, and the weaker ones catch the red too thick or too thin and finish short of baulk, which is a break-off you can do something with.

Two things to know. On Russian pyramid the opponent plays properly but rarely reaches eight balls, so treat a pyramid frame as practice. At bar billiards it is very strong: the black skittle only screens the 200 from a ball coming straight up the table, so the opponent scores the 200 from the side.
What the bars mean. Aiming is how close to the intended line they hit the ball. Power control is how accurately they judge the weight of a shot. Position is how much they play for the next ball rather than just the current one. Spin is how much backspin and topspin they are willing to use. Aggression is how likely they are to take a pot on instead of playing safe.

Practice and challenges

Two ways to play without an opponent.

Free practice gives you the table on your own with no rules and no scoring. Put the balls wherever you like and hit them.

Breaks are still counted at snooker. Practice has no rules, but it does keep your break: pot in the right order and the total climbs on the scoreboard and the referee calls it as he would in a frame — a red, then a colour, then a red, and once the reds are gone one colour of your choice and then the colours in order. Anything else ends it: two reds in two shots is two pots and not a break of two. Miss, go in-off, or pot out of turn and the referee says "end of break" and the count starts again from zero. Nothing is judged and nothing is penalised, so you can practise a break and still know what you built.
And you can play a clearance. AUTO RESPOT keeps the colours on the table while you practise, so you never run out of things to take after a red. Once the reds are gone it stops: the colour after the last red goes back up as it would in a frame, and from there the colours stay down and you can clear the table. Turn it off and practice follows the frame rules throughout.

The target

A chalk bullseye on the cloth to play the cue ball to: a 50 in the middle, then 20 and 10 rings, 26 cm across. Tap MENU in practice or a challenge and choose Target on; it is chalked where you last left it. When the cue ball comes to rest inside the target, the ring it stopped in — the 50, the 20 or the 10 — lights up. It goes out as soon as the cue ball moves again or you move the target.

Instant replays

Every shot is recorded as it happens. Watch the one that just went in from anywhere in the room, and keep the ones worth keeping.

After any shot, the Replay square on the pause menu plays it again — the swing of the cue included, not just the balls. The view is yours: the same controls that move the table move it during a replay, so you can watch from the pocket's end, from above, or from where your opponent stood. Hold the trigger for slow motion, press A to watch it again (or, with a real cue in your hand, press a thumbstick in), and point at KEEP THIS SHOT on the panel to save it — the row answers SAVED 3/128, and it will not save the same shot twice. Keeping a shot leaves the replay open, so you can watch it again after you have kept it.

The stroke and the delivery

A replay panel reads the stroke back to you: how hard, how much spin, and where the tip met the ball, with the cue ball drawn face on as you looked at it and, from above, how far it was pushed off your line by the side you used.

With a real cue, SHOW THE DELIVERY swaps that for THE DELIVERY: how straight your cue went through the ball, from the back of your last backswing to the end of the follow-through, measured off the line it was on at the strike. TOP DOWN shows it sideways, SIDE ON up and down, magnified to 20 mm either side of the line. It is drawn from the tracking at 500 samples a second, so a hard stroke is a smooth line, and practice swings before the real one are left out. SHOW THE STROKE goes back.

PREVIOUS SHOT and NEXT SHOT step through the frame, and PLAY THE BREAK plays your whole break back, one stroke after another.

The Replay gallery chip on the pause menu and Records → Replays on the main menu browse what you have kept, twelve to a page. DELETE THE ONE HIGHLIGHTED is a row on the panel, and it leaves the gallery open so you can carry on tidying up. A replay carries the table it was played on inside it, so a shot saved on one table still plays back on that table after you have moved on to another.

Balls

Twenty-seven sets. The BALLS panel opens a gallery: one rack, one table, one camera, so the only difference between two cards is the set.

Three of them weigh what they are made of. A phenolic resin ball is about 170 g at 57 mm, which is what every set here weighs except three: GLASS is 244 g, CHROME is titanium at 440 g, and TIMBER is lignum vitae at 122 g. A tip arriving at 5 m/s sends the wooden cue ball away at 7.1, the resin one at 6.6 and the titanium one at 4.7, and they carry that difference into every collision afterwards. Play a frame with the chrome set and it plays heavier.
Which is why you cannot swap to a heavier set mid-frame. Changing how the balls look half way through is free; changing what they weigh would move the goalposts on the shot you are lining up. A set that weighs the same as the one you are on is offered as normal; the others are dimmed until the frame is over. Online, both ends play the host's set, so the two of you are always on the same weight.
Some games insist. 9-ball and 10-ball need balls you can tell apart to play the sequence, so a set whose two halves are simply two colours is not offered. Pyramid and bar billiards use their own sets and nothing else, and the snooker games use snooker balls, so the row simply reads SNOOKER. When a game cannot show the set you picked it stands one in for the frame and says so in the row; your own choice is kept.

The jaws

An English pocket's facings are a four-point curve. It leaves the straight cushion nose 70 mm out from the pocket and arrives where the hole in the timber meets the frame's inner face, on a tangent set by the pocket's own centre line. A corner arrives at 45°, a middle at 10° off square. Where the curve meets the straight rail it is blended rather than cornered.

An American pocket is not curved at all: the facings are a straight mitre, cut at an angle to the rail. A Russian pyramid pocket is mitred as well, and so are the hexagon, octagon, round and L-shaped beds whenever the table asks for a mitre.

The knuckle on a mitred pocket

Where the two straight faces meet they do not meet in a point. Each one stops short and runs tangent into a small circle — 4 mm on the American tables, 5 mm at the corners and 11 mm at the middles of the Russian pyramid tables, or whatever KNUCKLE RADIUS is set to on a table you build — and the arc between the two tangent points is the knuckle. The circle is the whole of the tip, so a ball rebounds from the arc you can see, at the place you can see it.

You can hear how hard you caught it. A jaw tip has its own sound and it is scaled by the pace of the contact, a shade under a rail struck at the same speed — brushing one on the way past is a tick, rattling one at speed is a crack.

The rim

The rim is the flat, rounded cloth around the hole before the timber begins, and it is most of what gives a snooker pocket its look. On the snooker and English billiards tables it reaches 61.4 mm out from the centre at a corner and 67.4 mm at a middle; on the Chinese table 59.8 and 61.9 mm. The ball never has to fit through it: the opening is cushion and the rim is cloth, so a bigger rim does not make a pocket easier.

What that means when you play

  • The jaws take the pace off a firm pot instead of spitting it out, but a ball hit hard into a rounded snooker or Chinese pocket can still rattle between them and stay out. Speed is not free.
  • The cloth cut meets the drop exactly, on every table and at every pocket size, so a ball is never over bare slate before it is caught, and never caught while it is still on cloth.
  • The Chinese table is the tightest of the pool games at 1.50 balls, and the Russian tables are tighter than anything else here by a long way.

String pockets, and where a potted ball goes

A snooker table does not swallow a ball. It catches it in a bag and hands it back to you, and the whole run is built: a leather plate round the back of the pocket, a knotted string bag hanging from the plate, a brass ring at the bottom of the bag, and under the ring a wire collector running along the side of the table. The collector slopes.

  • It is the ball you potted, carrying the pace and the direction you potted it with, and it starts falling the moment it leaves the bed rather than waiting for the shot to finish.
  • The balls hit each other on the way in, and the row shifts along when a new one arrives. Every contact makes its own noise.
  • A full collector passes the ball on to an emptier one.
  • Respot a colour and it is lifted back out of the collector.
  • Replays show the pockets too, so you can watch a pot go down the bag again.

3-red, 6-red, 10-red and Chinese 8-ball are all built the same way. The other tables have a lined drop instead, which is why they sound the same whatever hits them.

  • Each shape remembers the rectangle's width, so flipping shape to look at something does not throw away a width you had dialled in.
  • Polygon and round beds have no middle pockets — the MIDDLE opening reads NONE. The L keeps its middles.

What the workshop tells you

At the bottom of the panel is the OPENING, given for the corners and the middles separately, in ball widths. It is the one figure you cannot set directly: it follows from pocket size, depth, jaw angle and softness together, and it is worked out against the widest ball on the table, cue ball included.

  • WONT FIT — the opening is less than one ball wide. It is a warning, not a refusal: the table still builds, still saves and still plays.
  • NONE — no pockets of that kind, as on a polygon.
  • TOO TIGHT TO BUILD — the cushions never reach the hole, so there is no opening to give.

Chalk

There is a block of chalk on the rail. You can pick it up, chalk your tip and put it back — and if you would rather not think about it, you never have to: your tip looks after itself unless you ask for the job.

Do I have to?

No. Settings → Chalk → CHALKING is set to AUTOMATIC, which means your tip is always chalked, nothing ever slips, and the chalk is simply an object on the rail you may pick up if you feel like it. Set it to MANUAL and it becomes part of the game: the tip wears a little with every stroke, wears a lot with a hard one, and a wide contact on a bare tip will miscue.

CHALK ON THE TABLE, on the same tab, can hide the block altogether while chalking is AUTOMATIC. Set chalking to MANUAL and it comes back.

Picking it up

Your bridge hand has to be free first. Slide it up the shaft and off the tip end with the side trigger held: the cue stays where it lay, frozen in your other hand, and the hand is yours. Reach for the block, pull the trigger within about ten centimetres of it, and hold it against your tip for a moment. Squeeze back onto the shaft to take the cue again.

With a real cue clamped to a controller the off hand is already free, so you can simply reach for it.

What a miscue does

The tip slides across the ball instead of gripping it. The ball leaves a long way off your aim — up to thirty degrees — with almost no spin on it, because an impulse through the middle of a ball has no arm to turn it with. Slip under the ball and it hops; slip over the top and the bed eats the stroke and it barely leaves. None of that is a dice roll: where your tip landed decides it, and the same contact gives the same answer every time. A tip that has only just lost the surface throws the ball less than a degree, which is a bad contact rather than a disaster.

Marks on the cloth

Chalk comes off the tip at impact and lands on the bed, so a frame leaves the table marked where the shots were played, as a real one does. A gentle push leaves a speck; a break throws a cloud. Sweep them away with your free hand laid on the cloth, trigger held. A new table is always a clean one.

Environment → Cloth has its own switch for whether the cloth picks chalk up at all, and a WIPE THE TABLE button.

Chalk on the white

The tip leaves chalk on the cue ball as well, on the spot it actually struck — a pale, tip-sized smudge in your chalk's colour. They build up over a session and fade over a minute or two, and the same hand that sweeps the cloth takes them off the ball: trigger held, palm over the white. Chalk → MARKS ON THE WHITE turns the whole thing off, and turning it off cleans the ball.

Holding it, and hearing it

Every sweep of the block across your tip is heard and felt — real recordings of a block on a tip, a different one each time, whether the tip needed chalking or not. CHALK SOUND turns that off if you would rather play in quiet.

Hold it however suits you. With the block in your hand, squeeze the side trigger: the block stops dead in mid-air while your hand keeps moving. Turn and slide your hand until the block sits the way you want it, then let go — that is how it is held from then on, this pickup and every one after. Two quick squeezes put it back to normal.

Chalk → CUSTOM CHALK POSITION turns this off, so the side trigger does nothing to the block, and turning it off puts the block back to normal. RESET CHALK POSITION does the same without turning it off.

KEEP IT IN REACH brings the block round the table with you. Walk to the other end and after a moment it moves to the rail beside you, on straight timber and never over a pocket. Turn it off and it stays where you left it.

Your block

CHALK BLOCK is a cube or a cylinder, and CHALK COLOUR green, grey or blue. The cube marks the cloth the way pressed chalk does; the cylinder is the hard sort and barely marks it at all. The marks come out in your chalk's own colour. Chalk is personal: online you see the block your opponent is using, in their colour, and how much is on their tip — and your own marks switch decides whether you see any marks at all, whoever made them.

Physics

Nothing in CueVR is scripted. Every pot, safety, swerve and rattle comes out of a simulation of real balls on real cloth, so the things you know from a real table work here, and the things you have to allow for on a real table you have to allow for here too.

Where you hit the cue ball

There is no spin control. What the cue ball does after it hits another ball is decided by two things only: where on the ball your tip lands, and how hard you hit it. (Players call these topspin "follow", backspin "screw" or "draw", and sidespin "side" or "english".)

  • Middle of the ball. The ball slides at first, picks up forward roll from the cloth as it travels, and carries on forward after it hits something.
  • Above the middle — topspin. The ball is already rolling forward when it arrives, so it keeps going through the ball it hits.
  • Below the middle — backspin. The ball arrives spinning backwards and comes back towards you after the contact. The cloth rubs backspin off as the ball travels, so it needs pace and it will not survive the length of the table: over a long distance the ball goes into normal forward roll before it ever arrives.
  • Middle, hit firmly — the stop shot. If the ball is still sliding rather than rolling when it arrives, it stops dead on a full contact instead of following through.
  • Left or right of the middle — sidespin. The ball spins like a top as it travels. This barely changes where the ball you hit goes, but it changes what your ball does off a cushion, and it lasts a couple of seconds before the cloth wears it off.

There is a limit to how far off centre you can hit, and the game applies it: past about 0.55 of the ball's radius a real tip slips off the ball — the miscue limit — so that is as much spin as anybody can put on. Aiming further out simply gives you the maximum rather than something impossible.

Sidespin pushes your ball off the line you aimed

Hit away from the middle of the ball and it does not set off down the line of the cue. It is pushed the opposite way to the sidespin you put on — right-hand sidespin sends it slightly left — and the harder you hit, the more it goes. This happens with a real cue too, and you have to aim off to allow for it. If you keep missing to one side when using sidespin, this is why.

How much it pushes it is yours to set. MENU → CONTROLS → CUE DEFLECTION, in degrees, from NONE to 5. Real cues run about half a degree for a carbon low-deflection shaft and around 2.3 for a plain one, and a traditional one-piece ash snooker cue is not a low-deflection shaft — the row labels those two so you have something to aim at. Above 3.2 it steps in 0.4 so you can find a number that matches your own cue rather than jumping past it.

Sidespin plus a raised cue makes the ball curve

Lift the back of the cue and some of the hit goes downwards into the cloth instead of along the table. With sidespin on, the ball sets off on one line and then bends away from it while the spin grips the cloth. A small lift gives a gentle curve you can use to get round a ball in the way; a big lift gives an obvious hook.

Cue angle

You cannot always hold the cue flat. When your ball is tight against a cushion the back of the cue has to come up to clear it, and the game does that for you rather than letting the cue pass through the cushion. A raised cue puts some of the hit downwards into the cloth, so expect less length and more curve than the same shot played flat.

Jump shots

Raise the cue far enough and the cue ball leaves the bed. It comes down, bounces two or three times — cloth over slate gives very little back — and settles into a normal roll. Snooker has its own rules about when a jump is allowed and when it is a foul, and the referee applies them.

Throw: the ball you hit does not go quite where you aimed

When two balls meet they grip each other for the instant they are touching, and that grip pushes the ball you hit slightly off the line. Two things cause it:

  • The angle. On anything other than a dead-straight contact the two balls rub across each other as they part, which drags the ball you hit a little fuller than the pure angle suggests.
  • Sidespin. A spinning cue ball rubs sideways against the ball it touches and pushes it that way — the same reason a spinning football bends. So a shot played with sidespin can miss even when the line was right, and the more sidespin you use the more you have to allow.

Both are small. On a short pot you will not notice; on a long one it is the difference between the middle of the pocket and the jaw.

Cushions

A cushion is rubber, not a wall, and it does three things you can use:

  • It takes more off a hard shot than a soft one. Restitution falls as the ball arrives faster, so a firm ball comes off proportionally slower than a gentle one. You cannot judge a two-cushion position from a one-cushion one just by hitting it harder.
  • Each table's rubber is different. An American pool cushion is the liveliest, a UK championship cushion the deadest, with snooker and Chinese 8-ball between them.
  • Sidespin changes the angle. The cushion has friction along it, so sidespin that is turning the same way the ball is travelling opens the angle out, and sidespin against it tightens the angle.

The cushion nose sits above the middle of the ball, as it does on a real table, so a ball is pressed down into the cloth as it goes in and lifted slightly as it comes out.

The American and Russian pyramid tables have K55 cushions, the profile those tables use: a lower, pointed nose, with the top of the cushion sloping down to it at 7 degrees.

The cue meets the table as it is drawn. The cushions, their slope, the wooden rail and the rail round the back of every pocket are solid to the cue at exactly the height you see them; the pocket holes are open down to their floor.

How much spin a tip off the middle gives. A rigid ball struck off centre takes spin in proportion to the offset, 2.5 times it as a ratio of spin to speed, and CueVR takes a share of that ideal for each kind of spin. Screw and top get 0.65 of it from a level cue, where the full figure made the draw shot come back further than players expected, rising to the whole of it as the butt comes up: from 15 degrees, reaching it at 45, so a steep massé or jump stroke gets all its screw. Side, and the swerve and massé a raised cue gives, keep the whole of it: turning those down with the screw made the big swerves and massé shots impossible, and on a real table they are not weaker than the rigid ideal says.

The cushions grip the ball with 1.25 times the friction they had before 6.5, so side and spin take a little more off a rail.

Jump shots. Raise the butt and strike down firmly and the ball leaves the bed. A ball struck near its middle jumps best; a massé with heavy side or screw jumps less, because more of the cue's push goes into spin, so it stays on the cloth and starts curving sooner.

Pockets

The pocket you see cut into the cloth is exactly the pocket that catches the ball. The jaws take the pace off a firm pot instead of spitting it out, but a ball hit hard into a rounded snooker or Chinese pocket can rattle between the jaws and stay out. Speed is not free. Every table's pocket is built to its own published specification, and they are very different from each other — see Pockets.

The drop is gravity. A ball that has cleared the jaws rolls over the cloth, turns over the pocket edge and falls off it. The lip is a real surface with the ball's weight acting along it, so the further round the edge the ball goes the faster it goes, as anything on a slope does. Whether a ball goes down at all is decided by the jaws and the cloth cut, not by how long it takes to disappear.

Bar billiards is the exception, because its nine holes are bored through the bed rather than cut into a rail. A ball goes down one by free fall across the hole, so it has to be rolled in: gently and it drops, hard and the far lip is still under its equator when it gets there and it skips across and carries on up the table.

The numbers behind it — the model, the constants and the formulae

Every ball is a rigid sphere with a position, a velocity and an angular velocity, stepped at a fixed 2000 Hz. Snooker balls are 52.5 mm across and 142 g; pool balls are 57.15 mm and 170 g. Gravity is 9.806 m/s². For a uniform sphere:

I = (2/5) · m · R²

Ball on cloth

The ball touches the cloth at one point beneath its centre, rc = (0, −R, 0). The velocity of that point is how much the ball is slipping rather than rolling:

u = v + ω × rc (horizontal part)

While it slips, kinetic friction acts against the slip. For a uniform sphere the linear and angular effects combine so the slip itself decays at

d|u|/dt = −(7/2) · μs · g μs = 0.20

The same force acts on the centre of mass, and through τ = rc × F on the spin — which is how backspin and topspin turn into a change of direction. Once the slip reaches zero the ball rolls, the spin is tied to the velocity, and only rolling resistance remains:

ωx = vz/R, ωz = −vx/R
dv/dt = −μr · g μr = 0.010

Side is about the vertical axis and is tied to nothing, so it decays on its own:

dωy/dt = −(5 · μsp · g) / (2R) μsp = 0.022

Ball on ball

An impulse along the line of centres, with a reduced mass of m/2 for equal balls:

Jn = −(1 + e) · vn / (2/m) e = 0.96

and a tangential impulse limited by friction between the surfaces (μ = 0.06). That second impulse is the throw described above, and it also transfers spin between the balls.

Cushions

The nose is set by the WPA specification at 63.5% of a ball's diameter:

h = 1.27 · R

so contact is above the equator. The rebound follows the impact model of Mathavan, Jackson and Parkin: the contact is integrated over accumulated normal impulse rather than resolved in one step, using energetic restitution. Restitution falls linearly with approach speed and is clamped at a floor:

e(v) = e₀ − k · v clamped to [0.55, e₀]

Leaving the bed

A ball off the cloth is under gravity, bounces with a restitution of 0.40, and settles once a hop would be under about a millimetre.

Pockets

Each pocket is a hole bored through the timber, with the cushion facings arriving on it and the cloth cut to meet it. An English facing is a four-point Bézier from the cushion face to the point where the bore meets the frame, on tangents fixed by the rail at one end and the pocket's own centre line at the other; an American or Russian one is a straight mitre. A ball is potted when its centre crosses into the drop circle — the circle you can see on the cloth. The lip is a quarter-circle roll, so a ball slower than

v < √( g · (l + R) ) l = lip radius

stays in contact and is steered down by it, while anything faster leaves the edge and flies.

The strike

One impulse along the cue's direction, applied where the tip touches. The offset is capped at 0.55 R, the miscue limit reported by Alciatore rather than the textbook half-ball. It puts the maximum spin at

ω·R / v = (5/2) · (b/R) = 1.37 b = 0.55 R

With the tip offset by (s, t) in units of the ball's radius:

r = right · (s · R) + up · (t · R)
J = cue_direction · (speed · m)
ω = (r × J) / I

Deflection is applied to the cue ball's direction as a function of the side used, and cue elevation tilts the impulse downwards, which is where swerve and jumps come from.
