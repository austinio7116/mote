Getting started

CueVR puts a full-size table in the room you are standing in. There is no shot meter and no aiming line: you hold a cue in both hands and play the shot.

You need a Quest 3 or 3S. You also need enough clear floor to walk along one side of a table. The game runs in passthrough, so you will see your own room around the table and can walk around your own furniture.

Your first frame

  1. Put the table somewhere in your room and set its height. See the next section.
  2. On the main menu, choose the game, the opponent and the match length.
  3. Point at BREAK OFF and pull the trigger.
  4. Pick up the cue with both hands and play.

Line the table up with real furniture. Set the height so the cloth matches something you actually have — a dining table, a desk, or the arm of a sofa. Then when you lean on the table your hand touches something solid. If the table is at a height that matches nothing in the room you will keep leaning on thin air, and the stroke will feel wrong.

A real snooker table has its bed about 85 cm off the floor, which is where the screen starts. You do not need a room big enough for the whole table. If space is tight, bring the part of the table you need into the space you have — the left stick slides it and the right stick turns it, at any time, and you can keep doing that as the frame goes on.

The table stays where you put it between sessions. You do not have to come back to this screen to move it, though: the sticks slide and turn the table whenever you are at it, including in the middle of a frame. The one thing only this screen can change is the height — to get back to it, press MENU and choose PLACE TABLE.

Controls

Both controllers hold the cue. The buttons do a small number of jobs, and the two side triggers are the ones worth learning.

The cue lies between your two controllers, so both hands aim it together: the line of the shot is the line between them. One hand is your bridge hand, where the front of the cue rests. The other is your butt hand, which holds the end and plays the stroke. Which physical controller is which depends on the handedness setting, so everything below is written in terms of the two hands rather than left and right.

Why the side triggers do not move your aim. Sliding your hand along a real cue does not move the cue. Holding either side trigger tells the game you are adjusting your hands rather than aiming, so the hand travels and the line you have set stays where it is.

Playing a shot

The cue is a real object lying between your two controllers. The line between your hands is the line of the shot, and how fast you move the butt hand is how hard the ball is hit.

Step by step

  1. Put the tip on the ball. Bring the tip up to the cue ball and line it up with where you want to hit.
  2. Choose your spot on the ball. Raise the tip to hit above centre, lower it to hit below. Move it left or right for side. There is no menu for this: it is simply where the tip is.
  3. Commit. Pull the trigger on your butt hand. This locks the line the cue will travel along, so the shot goes where you aimed it and not where your hands wander to during the delivery.
  4. Stroke through. With the trigger still held, move your butt hand forward through the ball. How fast you move it is how hard the ball is hit: a slow push rolls it gently, a break shot needs a real swing. Release the trigger afterwards.

Shots you cannot reach

Bring the shot to you. The sticks move the table at any time, in the middle of a frame included: the left stick slides it, the right stick turns it. Nothing needs to be opened first and nothing is interrupted. For a shot tight against a cushion, hold the butt hand's side trigger and pull that hand back to shorten the cue.

The games

Seven games, each on the table it is really played on. The rules below are the rules the game enforces — the referee applies all of them, so you never have to.

Snooker

12FT TABLE · 15 REDS · 6 COLOURS

Pot a red, then a colour, then another red, and so on. Each red you pot stays down; each colour goes back on its spot until the reds are finished. When the last red and the colour after it are gone, you clear the colours in order from yellow to black.

Values. Red 1, yellow 2, green 3, brown 4, blue 5, pink 6, black 7.

Nominating. When you are on a colour you have to say which one, and the game takes the colour you aim at.

Fouls are hitting nothing, hitting the wrong ball first, potting the cue ball, jumping the cue ball over a ball, and driving any ball off the table. The penalty is four points, or the value of the ball you were on, the ball you hit first, or the highest ball you potted — whichever of those is greatest. The points go to your opponent.

After a foul your opponent plays from where the balls are, or from in hand if the cue ball was potted. If the foul leaves them unable to hit the ball on, they get a free ball: they may nominate any ball as the one they are on, and it scores what the real ball on is worth.

Miss. If you fail to hit the ball on, a miss is called and your opponent may make you play the shot again from exactly where it was. Failing to escape a snooker counts — that is the most common miss there is. The only time a miss is not called is when your opponent needs snookers to win the frame anyway.

Three misses in a row lose the frame, but only genuine ones: a miss played from a snooker does not count towards it.

The end. Once the black is potted the frame is over. If the scores are level, the black is respotted, the player who did not pot it plays first from in hand, and the next score wins.

10-red snooker

10FT TABLE · 10 REDS · 6 COLOURS

Exactly the rules above — reds and colours, the same values, the same fouls, free balls, misses and respotted black — with ten reds instead of fifteen, on a ten-foot table.

A frame takes about half as long, and a total clearance is a realistic thing to try for rather than a fantasy.

6-red snooker

7FT TABLE · 6 REDS · 6 COLOURS

Snooker again, unchanged in every rule, with six reds on a seven-foot table.

Everything is within reach and frames are short, so this is the easiest way in if you have never played snooker, and the one to choose in a small room.

UK 8-ball

7FT TABLE · REDS AND YELLOWS · BLACK

The pub game. The table is open until somebody pots a colour, and that colour becomes theirs; the other player takes the rest. Clear your seven, then pot the black to win.

Fouls are potting the cue ball, hitting nothing, hitting the other player's colour first, hitting the black before you are on it, and driving a ball off the table. Jumping the cue ball is not a foul in pool.

Two shots. A foul gives your opponent two visits rather than ball in hand. The cue ball stays where it finished unless it was potted, in which case they play from in hand behind the baulk line. The first of those two shots is free: they may hit any ball. Once they pot one of their own, the second shot is gone.

The black. Potting it before your colours are gone loses the frame, as does fouling while potting it. If it goes in on the break it is put back and the break stands.

US 8-ball

9FT TABLE · SOLIDS AND STRIPES · 8 BALL

WPA rules. The table is open after the break — whatever you pot first decides whether you are solids or stripes — and once your group is clear you are on the 8.

Fouls are potting the cue ball, hitting nothing, hitting the other group first, hitting the 8 first while the table is open or before your group is clear, hitting anything other than the 8 once you are on it, and driving a ball off the table.

Ball in hand. Any foul gives your opponent the cue ball anywhere on the table — not just behind a line. It is a heavy penalty and it is meant to be.

The 8. Pot it after your group is clear and you win. Pot it early, or foul on the shot that pots it, and you lose. On the break it is simply put back.

9-ball

9FT TABLE · 9 BALLS · DIAMOND RACK

You must always hit the lowest numbered ball on the table first, but any ball may then go in. Pot the 9 legally and you win at once — including straight off the break, and including off a combination, which is what makes the game what it is.

Fouls are potting the cue ball, hitting nothing, hitting anything other than the lowest ball first, failing to pot a ball or send any ball to a cushion after the contact, and driving a ball off the table.

Ball in hand anywhere on the table for the opponent, as at US 8-ball. If the 9 was potted on a foul it goes back on its spot. Three fouls in a row loses the frame.

The push-out. The player who takes the first shot after the break may declare a push-out: they may hit the cue ball anywhere they like, with no need to hit the lowest ball or reach a cushion, and the only foul is potting the cue ball. A 9 potted on a push-out goes back. Afterwards their opponent decides whether to play the resulting position or hand it straight back.

Chinese 8-ball

10FT TABLE · SOLIDS AND STRIPES · ROUNDED POCKETS

Pool balls and WPA 8-ball rules — identical to US 8-ball above, including ball in hand anywhere for any foul — played on a ten-foot table built like a snooker table, with tight rounded pockets.

Those pockets are the whole difference. They reject a ball hit too hard and punish an approach that is even slightly off, so it plays as the hardest of the seven.

Match length is set on the main menu: one frame, or best of 3, 5, 7, 9 or 11. The break alternates between players and the score carries through the match. A player who cannot catch up will concede rather than play it out.

Rules and the referee

The rules are enforced for you, and called out loud. You do not have to keep score or remember what is on.

At snooker that includes reds and colours in the right order, colours going back on their spots, free balls after a foul, called misses, and the final clearance from yellow to black. At the pool games it includes your group, the two-shot carry at UK 8-ball, and the push-out at 9-ball.

When your opponent fouls

You are asked what you want to do. You can play on from where the balls have finished, or make your opponent play the shot again. If a miss was called and the table can be put back as it was, that is offered too. The referee announces what you chose.

Turning the referee off

The REF VOICE row on the main menu sets a female voice, a male voice, or no voice at all. The scoreboard stays either way.

What the bars mean. Aiming is how close to the intended line they hit the ball. Power control is how accurately they judge the weight of a shot. Career

A season of matches against the other players, with a league table.

You work up through the field as you win. Your progress is saved, so a season is something you can come back to over several sittings.

Practice and challenges

Two ways to play without an opponent.

Free practice gives you the table on your own with no rules and no scoring. Put the balls wherever you like and hit them.

Challenges set you a goal on a fixed layout — clear the table, or reach a score. Each one is judged over a single visit, so a miss ends the attempt and you start again.

Physics

Nothing in CueVR is scripted. Every pot, safety, swerve and rattle comes out of a simulation of real balls on real cloth, so the things you know from a real table work here, and the things you have to allow for on a real table you have to allow for here too.

Where you hit the cue ball

There is no spin control. What the cue ball does after it hits another ball is decided by two things only: where on the ball your tip lands, and how hard you hit it. (Players call these topspin "follow", backspin "screw" or "draw", and sidespin "side" or "english".)

  • Middle of the ball. The ball slides at first, picks up forward roll from the cloth as it travels, and carries on forward after it hits something.
  • Above the middle — topspin. The ball is already rolling forward when it arrives, so it keeps going through the ball it hits.
  • Below the middle — backspin. The ball arrives spinning backwards and comes back towards you after the contact. The cloth rubs backspin off as the ball travels, so it needs pace and it will not survive the length of the table: over a long distance the ball goes into normal forward roll before it ever arrives.
  • Middle, hit firmly — the stop shot. If the ball is still sliding rather than rolling when it arrives, it stops dead on a full contact instead of following through.
  • Left or right of the middle — sidespin. The ball spins like a top as it travels. This barely changes where the ball you hit goes, but it changes what your ball does off a cushion, and it lasts a couple of seconds before the cloth wears it off.

Sidespin pushes your ball off the line you aimed

Hit away from the middle of the ball and it does not set off down the line of the cue. It is pushed the opposite way to the sidespin you put on — right-hand sidespin sends it slightly left — and the harder you hit, the more it goes. This happens with a real cue too, and you have to aim off to allow for it. If you keep missing to one side when using sidespin, this is why.

Sidespin plus a raised cue makes the ball curve

Lift the back of the cue and some of the hit goes downwards into the cloth instead of along the table. With sidespin on, the ball sets off on one line and then bends away from it while the spin grips the cloth. A small lift gives a gentle curve you can use to get round a ball in the way; a big lift gives an obvious hook.

Cue angle

You cannot always hold the cue flat. When your ball is tight against a cushion the back of the cue has to come up to clear it, and the game does that for you rather than letting the cue pass through the cushion. A raised cue puts some of the hit downwards into the cloth, so expect less length and more curve than the same shot played flat.

Jump shots

Raise the cue far enough and the cue ball leaves the bed. It comes down, bounces two or three times — cloth over slate gives very little back — and settles into a normal roll. Snooker has its own rules about when a jump is allowed and when it is a foul, and the referee applies them.

Throw: the ball you hit does not go quite where you aimed

When two balls meet they grip each other for the instant they are touching, and that grip pushes the ball you hit slightly off the line. Two things cause it:

  • The angle. On anything other than a dead-straight contact the two balls rub across each other as they part, which drags the ball you hit a little fuller than the pure angle suggests.
  • Sidespin. A spinning cue ball rubs sideways against the ball it touches and pushes it that way — the same reason a spinning football bends. So a shot played with sidespin can miss even when the line was right, and the more sidespin you use the more you have to allow.

Both are small. On a short pot you will not notice; on a long one it is the difference between the middle of the pocket and the jaw.

Cushions

A cushion is rubber, not a wall, and it does three things you can use:

  • It takes more off a hard shot than a soft one. Restitution falls as the ball arrives faster, so a firm ball comes off proportionally slower than a gentle one. You cannot judge a two-cushion position from a one-cushion one just by hitting it harder.
  • Each table's rubber is different. An American pool cushion is the liveliest, a UK championship cushion the deadest, with snooker and Chinese 8-ball between them.
  • Sidespin changes the angle. The cushion has friction along it, so sidespin that is turning the same way the ball is travelling opens the angle out, and sidespin against it tightens the angle.

The cushion nose sits above the middle of the ball, as it does on a real table, so a ball is pressed down into the cloth as it goes in and lifted slightly as it comes out.

Pockets

The pocket you see cut into the cloth is exactly the pocket that catches the ball. The jaws take the pace off a firm pot instead of spitting it out, but a ball hit hard into a rounded snooker or Chinese pocket can rattle between the jaws and stay out. Speed is not free.

The numbers behind it — the model, the constants and the formulae

Every ball is a rigid sphere with a position, a velocity and an angular velocity, stepped at a fixed 2000 Hz. Snooker balls are 52.5 mm across and 142 g; pool balls are 57.15 mm and 170 g. Gravity is 9.806 m/s². For a uniform sphere:

I = (2/5) · m · R²

Ball on cloth

The ball touches the cloth at one point beneath its centre, rc = (0, −R, 0). The velocity of that point is how much the ball is slipping rather than rolling:

u = v + ω × rc (horizontal part)

While it slips, kinetic friction acts against the slip. For a uniform sphere the linear and angular effects combine so the slip itself decays at

d|u|/dt = −(7/2) · μs · g μs = 0.20

The same force acts on the centre of mass, and through τ = rc × F on the spin — which is how backspin and topspin turn into a change of direction. Once the slip reaches zero the ball rolls, the spin is tied to the velocity, and only rolling resistance remains:

ωx = vz/R, ωz = −vx/R
dv/dt = −μr · g μr = 0.010

Side is about the vertical axis and is tied to nothing, so it decays on its own:

dωy/dt = −(5 · μsp · g) / (2R) μsp = 0.022

Ball on ball

An impulse along the line of centres, with a reduced mass of m/2 for equal balls:

Jn = −(1 + e) · vn / (2/m) e = 0.96

and a tangential impulse limited by friction between the surfaces (μ = 0.06). That second impulse is the throw described above, and it also transfers spin between the balls.

Cushions

The nose is set by the WPA specification at 63.5% of a ball's diameter:

h = 1.27 · R

so contact is above the equator. The rebound follows the impact model of Mathavan, Jackson and Parkin: the contact is integrated over accumulated normal impulse rather than resolved in one step, using energetic restitution. Restitution falls linearly with approach speed and is clamped at a floor:

e(v) = e₀ − k · v clamped to [0.55, e₀]

Leaving the bed

A ball off the cloth is under gravity, bounces with a restitution of 0.40, and settles once a hop would be under about a millimetre.

Pockets

Each pocket is an arc cut into the cloth with straight jaw legs, a rounded lip and a throat behind it. A ball is potted when its centre crosses into the drop circle — the circle you can see on the cloth. The lip is a quarter-circle roll, so a ball slower than

v < √( g · (l + R) ) l = lip radius

stays in contact and is steered down by it, while anything faster leaves the edge and flies.

The strike

One impulse along the cue's direction, applied where the tip touches. With the tip offset by (s, t) in units of the ball's radius:

r = right · (s · R) + up · (t · R)
J = cue_direction · (speed · m)
ω = (r × J) / I

Deflection is applied to the cue ball's direction as a function of the side used, and cue elevation tilts the impulse downwards, which is where swerve and jumps come from.
